# The exact screen after training — corrected player review

## Scope and evidence

This review is only about the first-time transition immediately after training, not the later story chapters. Fresh desktop1280×720 and portrait390×844 profiles were used. The training course was completed using the existing automated simulation bot, not skipped and not completed by a human tester. The application then performed its own completion handoff. From that handoff onward, the browser clock was unmodified: hands off first, then one actual mouse drag. No later cutscene was launched manually. No game source was changed.

84 transition screenshots were inspected across seven contact sheets, with frames1,8 and51 also examined at full size. Two additional screenshots preserve the completed training course. The first observation begins after the completion condition was detected, not at an exact cutscene-start timestamp. Portrait is a viewport check using mouse input, not a physical-phone touch test. Audio was not assessed. The saved manifest records image positions, observation times and console logs; full run-state snapshots did not serialize and are absent. Screenshots are the basis of the findings below.

## What this screen actually says to me

1. A huge gold crown-shaped face appears over the arena, above a glowing green zigzag. A smaller copy of that face is already visible toward the upper right. I do not know whether either is a character, a boss, a logo or decoration.
2. A bright cream-colored flash and branching cracks fill much of the screen. Something happened, but I cannot see who caused it or what changed because of it.
3. Three white shapes drop through the arena. “CUT.” appears, followed by “CUT THE WORLD.” These sound dramatic but do not identify a target.
4. The big imagery disappears. The small player remains on a platform below two red triangles, a short green zigzag and the small gold face. No visible instruction remains to tell me what this arrangement means or what action continues it.
5. I wait. The same composition persists. A drag finally launches the character and starts a much busier battlefield.

## Player verdict

**This is an unclear interruption between learning the controls and actually using them.** It neither tells a legible story nor clearly starts the game. My likely questions are “Am I supposed to watch?”, “Did it freeze?”, “Do I cut the green line?”, and “Is the crown my target?”

I should not have praised its visual identity as if that answered the complaint. The actual post-training experience needs to make sense before its style matters.

## Specific problems visible in the captures

### The screen asks me to interpret symbols I have not learned

The gold face, green line, falling shapes and fractures have no clear cause-and-effect relationship. A face getting bigger and disappearing is not, by itself, an understandable event. I cannot infer a friendship, a trap or a rescue from this sequence.

Evidence: frames1–6 and43–49.

### The player is not the visual subject

The giant face dominates the opening image while the little playable character is dark and low in the frame. In the settled screen, the score is huge while the character and short green line are small. My attention is directed toward things whose meaning is unclear, not toward what I can do next.

Evidence: full-size frames1 and8; portrait frame51.

### The flash supplies intensity without explanation

The bright frame obscures much of the underlying scene. Afterward, the arena does not present an obvious, readable before-and-after change. I see a screen effect, not a concrete event I can describe.

Evidence: frames2–4 and45–46. This is a readability judgment, not a photosensitivity assessment.

### The quiet screen looks like unfinished playback

Desktop frame8, at2.407seconds into observation, already has the settled composition. It remains substantially the same through frame30 at9.947seconds. The first drag begins at10.246seconds, and frame31 shows the character airborne with many more enemies present. Portrait shows a comparable hands-off wait until the drag at8.795seconds.

Waiting for the first action can be perfectly reasonable. **Failing to communicate that the game is waiting for me is the problem.** There is no persistent, visible instruction on the settled screen explaining where to aim or what the green seam does. The capture cannot establish how often real players would hesitate, but it clearly shows the missing handoff information.

### Portrait stretches the ambiguity

The small face is near the top, the green line and enemies are much farther down, and the player sits below them. Their spatial relationship is harder to read. The oversized score remains prominent. More vertical space has not produced a clearer composition.

Evidence: full-size frame51 and sheets5–6.

## Focused replacement plan — only this transition

### 1. Decide what this moment is

Do not mix a symbolic movie, an unexplained frozen battlefield and a hidden first-action requirement. Make it a short, playable introduction: **the player is blocked; Watcher opens a seam; the player cuts through it.** If the screen cannot show that clearly, remove the symbolic sequence and go straight to a readable first encounter.

### 2. Put cause, action and result on the same screen

Keep Cutter, Watcher and the barrier in one readable composition. Watcher visibly pulls the barrier apart. A small opening appears directly in front of Cutter. The opening should look cuttable and use the targeting language just taught in training. Remove the unrelated falling-shape montage and duplicate large/small face presentation from this beat.

### 3. Make the handoff unmistakable

Settle on the actual playable character and the actual opening. Keep a plain instruction visible until the action happens: “CUT THROUGH THE OPENING.” Use the appropriate mouse/touch/key hint underneath. Animate a restrained directional cue from the player toward the target. Do not leave the player wondering whether a tiny zigzag is decorative.

Do not auto-launch merely because the player hesitates. Keep the scene visibly alive and the invitation readable; strengthen the same cue after a wait instead of adding unrelated messages.

### 4. Reward that first cut with a visible consequence

The cut should split the barrier at the point of contact, take Cutter through, and reveal the first fight. Bring the combat HUD back after the event is understood. Introduce pressure gradually enough that the player can connect their action to the result. A future sacrifice/rescue plot must not be implied here unless the animation actually shows it.

### 5. Validate the replacement against this exact failure

Capture the complete training-to-game transition again, not an isolated replay. Inspect desktop and portrait at normal speed. Wait ten seconds without input: the screen must still clearly invite the next action. Perform that action: it must visibly affect the identified target. Check that training's last input cannot accidentally skip or trigger it. Ask unfamiliar players “What is happening?” and “What would you do next?” without explaining the intended story.

## Bottom line

**Replace this confusing handoff before expanding the rest of the story.** The current sequence gives me a large symbol, a flash, a slogan and then an unexplained wait. It needs to give me a situation, an obvious action and a visible result.
